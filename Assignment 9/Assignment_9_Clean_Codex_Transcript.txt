This step helps us understand what the model is trying to predict and gives us context for judging its predictions later.

| Statistic | What it tells us | Why it is useful |

| Count | Number of students with a recorded exam score | Shows how many usable target values we have. Comparing it with the total row count reveals missing scores. |
| Mean | Average exam score | Gives a reference for a typical score and supports the simple average-score baseline we discussed. |
| Median | Middle score when scores are ordered | Helps describe the center without being strongly affected by unusually high or low scores. |
| Standard deviation | How spread out scores are around the mean | Helps us understand how much students’ scores vary and puts prediction errors into context. |
| Minimum | Lowest recorded score | Shows the bottom of the observed range and helps identify values worth checking. |
| Maximum | Highest recorded score | Shows the top of the observed range and helps identify values worth checking. |

Comparing the mean and median can also be useful. If they differ considerably, unusually high or low scores may be pulling the mean in one direction. The histogram helps us investigate that.

A histogram groups scores into intervals—for example, 60–69, 70–79, and 80–89—and shows how many students fall into each interval. It helps answer questions such as:

- Where do most scores fall?
- Are scores tightly clustered or widely spread?
- Is there a long tail toward lower or higher scores?
- Are there unusual gaps, multiple clusters, or many scores at the maximum?

These details matter because a model might perform well for common scores while making larger mistakes on the few students at either extreme. An overall average error can hide that pattern.

One distinction to keep in mind: these statistics describe the target; they do not tell us which features predict it well. Also, when we later build the baseline, we’ll calculate its mean using only the training scores.

CODEX:
The split lets us teach the model using one group of students and evaluate its predictions on another group.

With your 250 students, an 80% / 20% split gives us:

- Training set: 200 students. The model receives their features and actual exam scores, then learns relationships between them.
- Test set: 50 students. After training, the model predicts their scores using their features. We compare those predictions with their actual scores to measure errors.

Keeping the test students separate helps us assess generalization: how well the model predicts for students it did not learn from. A model can fit its training examples closely and still make poor predictions for new students.

We must keep each student’s features paired with their exam score during the split. This will produce four objects: X_train, X_test, y_train, and y_test.

random_state=42 makes the random split reproducible. The splitting function shuffles the rows before selecting the groups. The number 42 acts as a fixed starting point, called a seed, for that random process.

With the same data in the same row order and the same seed, rerunning the code gives us the same training and test students. This makes debugging easier and lets us compare models using consistent groups.

42 has no special statistical advantage. Another integer would produce a different, repeatable split. Also, this setting controls the randomness of the split; it does not automatically control every later model’s randomness.

The split also prepares us for preprocessing: we’ll learn replacement values for missing data and any scaling rules from the training set, then apply those same rules to the test set.

Preprocessing turns our selected features into a numerical form the regression model can use. We’ll handle missing values, different measurement scales, and text categories.

We need two separate paths because numerical and categorical features require different treatment.

1. Numerical features: fill missing values → scale

   SimpleImputer fills missing entries. A reasonable starting choice is the median of each training column. For example, a missing study-hours value would receive the training students’ median study hours. This replacement is an estimate, rather than the student’s known actual value. Imputation documentation

   StandardScaler then standardizes each numerical column:

   scaled value = (value − training mean) / training standard deviation

   For example, if training study hours had a mean of 8 and a standard deviation of 2:

   - 10 hours becomes 1.
   - 8 hours becomes 0.
   - 6 hours becomes −1.

   This puts measurements such as hours, minutes, and attendance percentages on comparable scales. Values can be positive or negative; standardization does not restrict them to 0–1. Scaling documentation

2. Categorical features: fill missing values → encode

   We can use SimpleImputer to fill missing categories with the most common training-set category. The literal "None" in programming experience remains a valid category meaning no experience. Imputation documentation

   OneHotEncoder creates numerical indicator columns for the categories. For example:

   | Study group | Indicator for No | Indicator for Yes |

   | No | 1 | 0 |
   | Yes | 0 | 1 |

   This represents category membership without assigning arbitrary numerical distances between categories. Encoding documentation

3. Combine the paths into one workflow

   - Pipeline chains steps in order, such as imputation followed by scaling.
   - ColumnTransformer sends the five numerical columns through their pipeline and the two categorical columns through theirs, then combines the results.

   Later, we can put that combined preprocessor and the regression model into one larger pipeline. Pipeline documentation

Fitting preprocessing means learning its rules. It learns the replacement values, scaling statistics, and category mappings from X_train. We then transform X_test using those same learned rules. Calculating fresh rules from the test set would let test information influence the workflow. Training/test preprocessing guidance

Our target, y, stays as the original numerical exam scores in this workflow.

numeric_features = [

    "sleep_hours_per_night",

    "commute_minutes",

    "prior_programming_experience",

    ("imputer", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
    ("scaler", StandardScaler()),
])

categorical_pipeline = Pipeline(steps=[
    ("imputer", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
    ("encoder", OneHotEncoder(
        handle_unknown="ignore",
        sparse_output=False,
    )),
])

# Apply the appropriate pipeline to each group of columns.
preprocessor = ColumnTransformer(transformers=[
    ("numeric", numeric_pipeline, numeric_features),
    ("categorical", categorical_pipeline, categorical_features),
])

# Learn preprocessing rules from training data and apply them.
X_train_processed = preprocessor.fit_transform(X_train)

# Apply the same learned rules to test data.
X_test_processed = preprocessor.transform(X_test)

print(f"Processed training features: {X_train_processed.shape}")
print(f"Processed test features: {X_test_processed.shape}")

Here’s how each part works:

1. The feature lists identify which columns follow each path.
   We have five numerical features and two categorical features. ColumnTransformer selects them by their column names.

2. numeric_pipeline runs two steps in order.
   SimpleImputer(strategy="median") learns each numerical column’s training median and uses it to fill missing values. Then StandardScaler learns the mean and standard deviation of the filled training columns and standardizes them.

   Each tuple contains a step name, the pipeline to use, and the columns it should process. It combines their outputs into one numerical feature array.

5. fit_transform(X_train) learns and applies the rules.
   It learns replacement values, scaling statistics, and category mappings from the training students, then produces their processed features.

6. transform(X_test) applies those existing rules.
   It processes the test students using the rules already learned from training.

The four metrics describe its performance in different ways:

| Metric | What it calculates | How to interpret it |

| MAE — Mean Absolute Error | Average size of the errors, ignoring their signs | Measured in exam-score points. An MAE of 8 means predictions are off by 8 points on average. Lower is better. |
| MSE — Mean Squared Error | Average of the squared errors | Gives large mistakes more weight. Its units are points squared. Lower is better. |
| RMSE — Root Mean Squared Error | Square root of MSE | Returns the result to exam-score points while retaining sensitivity to large mistakes. Lower is better. |
| R² — R squared | Compares squared prediction errors with the variation in actual test scores | Measures improvement relative to predicting the test-set mean. Higher is better. |

For R²:

- 1 means perfect predictions.
- 0 means the same squared error as predicting the test-set mean for everyone.
- A negative value means worse performance than that reference. R² documentation

There is a small distinction here: our baseline predicts the training mean, while R²’s reference uses the test mean. Consequently, our baseline’s test R² can be slightly negative rather than exactly zero.

Later, we’ll evaluate the regression model on the same test students with the same metrics. For example, if baseline MAE were 8 and model MAE were 5, the model would reduce average absolute error by 3 exam-score points. That gives us a concrete way to assess whether learning from student features helped.

Your understanding is correct: linear regression predicts a score by multiplying each input by a learned weight, then adding those contributions together. It also learns a constant called the intercept:

\[
\text{predicted score}=b+w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\cdots+w_px_p
\]

Here, \(x\) represents feature values, \(w\) represents their weights, and \(b\) is the intercept. Linear-model documentation

For a numerical feature, a positive weight means increasing that feature increases the prediction, holding the other inputs fixed. A negative weight means it decreases the prediction.

With one feature, this equation describes a straight line. With two features, it describes a plane. Our model will combine 10 processed input columns, because one-hot encoding expanded the original seven features.

How does it find the weights?

It chooses weights and an intercept that make the total squared training error as small as possible. This is called ordinary least squares.

For any proposed set of weights, we can:

1. Calculate a prediction for each training student.
2. Subtract that prediction from the student’s actual score.
3. Square each difference.
4. Add those squared differences together.

For example, predicting 75 when the actual score is 80 contributes:

\[
(80-75)^2=25
\]

The model seeks the weights that minimize this total across all 200 training students. Minimizing that sum also minimizes training MSE, because MSE divides it by the fixed number of students. Least-squares documentation

What actually happens inside scikit-learn?

For the numerical array we prepared, LinearRegression uses a matrix least-squares solver. It treats the feature table as a matrix and finds a weighted combination of its columns that comes closest to the actual-score column.

The solver uses a mathematical technique called singular value decomposition (SVD), which breaks the matrix problem into simpler parts. It solves for the coefficients jointly. It does not assign each weight independently from that feature’s correlation, and this implementation does not require repeatedly making small gradient-descent updates. Scikit-learn implementation details

The weights describe relationships learned from the training data; they do not establish that a feature caused a score change. Also, because we scaled numerical inputs, a one-unit increase in a scaled feature represents one training standard deviation.

This connects directly to our baseline: the baseline uses one constant prediction. Linear regression learns how feature contributions can adjust predictions for individual students. We’ll then check whether those adjustments reduce errors on the test set.

In this illustrative example, changing the weight tilts the line, while changing the intercept moves it up or down. “Find least-squares fit” selects the combination with the smallest total squared error for these four example students.

Every metric starts with the difference between an actual score and a prediction. For example, imagine these two students:

| Actual score | Predicted score | Error: actual − predicted |

| 80 | 78 | +2 |

The model predicted too low for the first student and too high for the second.

1. MAE: How many points are predictions off by, on average?

   Take the size of each error, ignoring its sign, then average:

   \[
   MAE=\frac{2+8}{2}=5
   \]

   Here, predictions are off by 5 exam-score points on average. Ignoring signs prevents errors in opposite directions from cancelling each other.

   Lower MAE is better.

2. MSE: How large are the errors when we give bigger mistakes more weight?

   Square each error before averaging:

   \[
   MSE=\frac{2^2+(-8)^2}{2}

   \]

3. RMSE: Can we express that squared-error measure in exam-score points?

   Take the square root of MSE:

   \[
   RMSE=\sqrt{34}\approx5.83
   \]

   RMSE is expressed in exam-score points, while retaining sensitivity to large mistakes. That is why it can be larger than MAE for the same predictions.

   Lower RMSE is better.

4. R²: How does squared-error performance compare with predicting the mean?

   R² compares the model’s squared errors with the errors from predicting the test-set mean for everyone:

   - 1: perfect predictions.
   - 0: the same squared error as that mean prediction.
   - Negative: worse than that mean prediction.

   Higher R² is better. It is not the percentage of students whose scores were predicted correctly. R² documentation

- Points far from zero: students with large prediction errors.

Linear Regression uses one weighted formula. For example, its study-hours coefficient stays the same across students, holding the other modeled features fixed.

A Random Forest Regressor combines many regression decision trees. Each tree learns a sequence of questions about feature values. An illustrative tree might ask:

- Is the student’s quiz average above 80?
- Within that group, is attendance above 90%?
- Within that smaller group, how many practice problems did they complete?

The tree predicts a numerical score based on the training students who reach the same final group. With the default squared-error setting, that prediction is their average score. Scikit-learn’s RandomForestRegressor documentation.

The forest trains trees on different random samples of the training data, then averages their predictions. For example, predictions of 75, 80, and 85 would average to 80. Combining many trees makes predictions less dependent on the particular decisions of one tree. Scikit-learn’s explanation of random forests.

This makes the model nonlinear: it can learn relationships that change across ranges and combinations of features. For instance, it could use study hours differently depending on quiz performance or practice activity. We’ll test whether that flexibility helps on our held-out students.
